Professional Introduction

Professional Self-Assessment

Completing the Computer Science program at Southern New Hampshire University and developing this ePortfolio have given me an opportunity to reflect on how much I have grown as a student and future software developer. When I began the program, I was mainly focused on learning how to make programs work. As I progressed, I learned that successful software development involves much more than writing functional code. It requires planning, testing, communication, security awareness, database management, and an understanding of the people who will use the finished product.

My coursework helped shape my professional goal of entering the software-development field. Working with languages including Python, Java, C++, and JavaScript introduced me to different approaches to programming. Learning multiple development environments taught me how to adapt instead of relying on only one technology. I became more confident in reading unfamiliar code, identifying problems, researching possible solutions, and testing changes until an application worked as intended.

Collaboration was another important part of my development. Throughout the program, I participated in class discussions, considered different perspectives, responded to feedback, and explained technical decisions to classmates and instructors. My software-development lifecycle coursework showed me how Agile practices can help a team organize responsibilities, prioritize requirements, and respond to change. My professional experience as a retail team lead has also strengthened these abilities because I regularly communicate with team members, address concerns, and adjust plans based on the needs of employees and customers.

I also improved my ability to communicate with stakeholders who have different levels of technical knowledge. Through narratives, presentations, code reviews, diagrams, and project documentation, I learned how to explain technical information clearly without making it unnecessarily complicated. My CS 499 code review required me to examine the original Inventory Tracker, identify weaknesses, and explain my planned enhancements in an understandable way.

My understanding of software engineering and design grew significantly throughout the program. Projects involving object-oriented programming, mobile development, graphics, and software testing taught me how to organize code into manageable components. The Inventory Tracker gave me an opportunity to apply these skills by refining the login and registration process, strengthening input validation, improving the inventory display, and making the Add Item and Edit Item features easier to use.

Algorithms and data structures helped me understand how the organization of data affects an application's efficiency and functionality. I worked with arrays, lists, dictionaries, collections, searching, sorting, and conditional processing. In the enhanced Inventory Tracker, I used these concepts to improve how inventory records were retrieved, validated, displayed, searched, and organized.

Database development became one of the most practical areas of my coursework. I learned how applications create, read, update, and delete records and why validation, unique identifiers, and properly structured queries matter. The Inventory Tracker database supports user accounts and inventory records. Its database enhancements made the add, edit, display, and delete operations more dependable.

Security has also become a regular part of how I evaluate software. My secure-software coursework taught me about input validation, authentication, dependency vulnerabilities, and data protection. I applied this mindset to the Inventory Tracker by considering how user credentials and inventory information should be validated and protected, as well as how private configuration files and device-specific information should be kept out of a public repository.

I selected the Inventory Tracker for all three enhancement categories because it demonstrates how software design, algorithms, and databases work together in one complete application. The original artifact represents my starting point, while the enhanced version shows my ability to evaluate existing code, identify weaknesses, and create meaningful improvements. Together, the artifacts, narratives, and code review demonstrate my readiness to begin a career in software development and continue growing as technologies and industry expectations change.
